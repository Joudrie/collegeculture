College Culture Basics

When to do what, how to visit, and what people get wrong.

The timeline · Visiting a campus · Myths you will hear

The timeline

Typical dates. Every school sets its own deadlines, so check them.

Junior year, fall and winter
  • Take the PSAT in October. It is practice for the SAT, and juniors with top scores can qualify for National Merit.
  • Grades from junior year matter a lot. It is the last full year colleges see before you apply.
Junior year, spring
  • Take the SAT or ACT if the schools you like want or accept scores. Many people take it twice.
  • Start a list of around 10 schools: a few reaches, a few matches, a few likelies. The quiz can help.
  • Visit a couple of schools if you can, even nearby ones you are not sure about. Virtual tours count too.
  • Ask two teachers for recommendation letters before summer. Pick teachers who know you, not the ones with the best grade.
Summer before senior year
  • The Common App usually opens August 1. Make an account and fill in the easy parts.
  • Draft your main essay. It will take more rewrites than you think.
  • Write down every deadline in one place.
Senior year, fall
  • The FAFSA usually opens October 1. File it early, since some aid runs out. Some private schools also want the CSS Profile.
  • Early Decision and Early Action deadlines are often November 1 or 15.
  • Retake the SAT or ACT in the fall if you want a higher score.
Senior year, winter
  • Regular Decision deadlines are mostly in early January.
  • Your school sends midyear grades, so keep them up.
  • Apply for outside scholarships. Local ones get fewer applicants.
Senior year, spring
  • Decisions come out from March to early April.
  • Compare financial aid offers side by side. If one is much worse, you can ask the school to take another look.
  • Go to an admitted students day if you can.
  • Commit by May 1. Do not let your grades slide, since schools can take back an offer.
Summer after
  • Fill out housing forms, sign up for orientation and take any placement tests.
  • You will probably get your roommate assignment. Say hi before move-in.

Visiting a campus

Before you go
  • Sign up for the official tour and info session on the school's website. They fill up in spring and fall.
  • Go when classes are in session if you can. An empty summer campus feels nothing like the real thing.
  • Check if the school offers travel help. Some pay for visits for lower-income students.
On the tour
  • Tour guides are paid to love the school. Listen, but don't treat it as the whole story.
  • Ask your guide what they would change about the school. A real answer tells you a lot.
  • Ask how hard it is to get the classes you want, and how big intro classes really are.
  • Ask about housing after first year, and how many people move off campus.
After the tour
  • Eat in a dining hall. It is the fastest way to see what students are actually like.
  • Walk off the tour route: the library, the student center, the town right next to campus.
  • Read the bulletin boards and flyers. They show what students really do on a Tuesday night.
  • Talk to a random student if they look free. Ask what a normal weekend looks like.
Can't visit?
  • Most schools have virtual tours and online info sessions.
  • Visit a nearby school of the same type, like a small liberal arts college or a big state school, to get a feel for it.
  • Admitted students days in April are a great time to visit if you get in.
Write it down
  • Right after each visit, write down three things you liked and one thing that bugged you. Schools blur together fast.

Myths you will hear

"You need to know your major before you start."
Lots of students change majors, and most schools let you start undeclared. Programs like nursing and engineering are the exception, since they want you to start on day one.
"Private colleges are too expensive for my family."
Look at the price after aid, not the sticker price. Some pricey private schools cost lower-income families less than a state school. Every school page here shows both.
"Everyone gains the freshman 15."
Research says most first-years gain a few pounds, not fifteen.
"Community college is for people who could not get in anywhere."
It is often the cheapest path to a four-year degree. Many states have deals that make transferring credits to public universities easier.
"You need a huge list of activities."
A few things you really care about beat ten clubs you barely went to. A job counts too.
"Your essay has to be about something tragic."
It does not. Everyday topics work great if they show who you are and how you think.
"A gap year will hurt your chances."
It usually does not. Many schools let admitted students push their start back a year if they ask.
"Rankings tell you which school is best."
Rankings weigh things like money spent and reputation surveys. They do not know what you want out of college.
"If you get into a top school, you are set for life."
For most careers, what you do in college matters more than the name. Check graduation rates and pay on each school page.
"Top schools only take perfect students."
Selective schools read the whole application. Perfect scores do not guarantee anything, and a B does not rule you out.
"Most colleges are super hard to get into."
Most colleges admit most of the people who apply. The super selective ones are a small slice.
"Big lectures mean nobody will help you."
Big classes usually come with smaller discussion sections, TAs and office hours. You have to show up and ask.
"AP credits mean you will graduate early."
Every school has its own rules. Some give credit for a 4 or 5, some only use APs to skip intro classes.
"Your roommate will be your best friend."
Maybe. Lots of roommates are just friendly, and that is fine. If things go badly, your RA can help, and many schools let you switch.
