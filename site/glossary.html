Applying

Common App
One online application that most colleges accept. You fill in your info and write one main essay, then send it to many schools.
Supplemental essays
Extra short essays a specific school asks for on top of the main one. "Why us?" is the classic.
Early Decision (ED)
Apply early, usually in November, and hear back early. It is binding: if you get in, you have to go and pull your other applications. Only do it for a clear first choice, and run the net price calculator first.
Early Action (EA)
Apply early and hear back early, but it is not binding. You can still compare offers and decide by May 1.
Restrictive Early Action
Also called Single-Choice Early Action. Not binding, but you usually cannot apply early to other private schools at the same time. Rules differ by school.
Regular Decision (RD)
The normal deadline, usually around January. Decisions come out around March or April.
Rolling admission
The school reviews applications as they come in and decides as it goes. Applying earlier usually helps.
Deferred
You applied early, and the school pushed your application into the regular round instead of deciding right away.
Waitlist
Not a yes or a no yet. The school may offer spots later in spring. Commit somewhere else by the deadline in the meantime.
May 1
National College Decision Day. Most schools want your answer and a deposit by then.
Deposit
A payment that holds your spot once you commit. It usually goes toward your first bill.
Test-optional
You choose whether to send SAT or ACT scores. Some schools have gone back to requiring them, so check each school.
Test-blind
The school will not look at scores at all, even if you send them. Rare.
Admit rate
The share of applicants who get in. A low rate means hard to get into, not that the school is right for you.
Holistic review
The school looks at the whole application: grades, classes, essays, activities and recommendations, not just numbers.
Demonstrated interest
Some schools track whether you visited, opened their emails or went to an info session. Others ignore it completely.
Legacy
An applicant whose parent went to the school. Some schools give a boost for it and some have stopped.
Reach, match, likely
How the quiz sorts your chances. Reach: getting in is a long shot. Match: your record fits. Likely: you are above their usual admit.

Paying

Sticker price
The full listed price for a year: tuition, housing, food and books. Also called cost of attendance. Most students on aid pay less.
Net price
What you actually pay after grants and scholarships. This is the number to compare.
Net price calculator
A tool every college has to post on its website. Plug in your family's info and it estimates your real price.
FAFSA
The free federal form for financial aid. Almost every school uses it. It opens in the fall of senior year, so file early.
CSS Profile
An extra aid form some private colleges use. It asks more detailed money questions. There is a fee, but many students get it waived.
Student Aid Index (SAI)
The number the FAFSA gives you that estimates what your family can pay. It replaced the old EFC.
Grant
Free money you do not pay back. The Pell Grant is the federal one for families with lower income.
Scholarship
Free money for grades, talent or need. Some come from the school, some from outside groups.
Merit aid
Money for grades or talent, no matter what your family earns. Many of the most selective schools give none and only give need-based aid.
Need-based aid
Money based on what your family can afford.
Loan
Borrowed money you pay back with interest. Federal student loans usually have better terms than private ones.
Work-study
A federal aid program that gives you a part-time job, usually on campus, to help pay.
Meets full need
The school promises to cover the gap between its price and what the aid formula says your family can pay. The package can still include loans and work-study.
Need-blind
Whether you can pay does not affect whether you get in. Need-aware means it can. Need-blind does not always mean the school meets full need.
In-state vs. out-of-state
Public colleges charge much less to students from their own state.
Tuition Break
A New England program that can cut out-of-state tuition at public colleges in the other New England states for some majors.

Types of schools

Liberal arts college
A small school that is mostly undergrads, with small classes and a broad education across subjects. Think Williams or Bates.
University
A school with grad programs too. Usually bigger, with more majors and more research going on.
Public vs. private
Public schools are funded by the state and cost less for in-state students. Private schools set their own price but often give more aid.
Community college
A two-year school with low tuition. You can earn an associate degree and then transfer to a four-year school.
Ivy League
An athletic conference of eight old schools: Brown, Columbia, Cornell, Dartmouth, Harvard, Penn, Princeton and Yale. Now mostly used to mean prestige.
NESCAC
An athletic conference of small New England liberal arts colleges, like Amherst, Bowdoin, Middlebury and Williams.
Division I, II, III
NCAA levels for college sports. D-I is the biggest and most intense. D-III schools do not give athletic scholarships.
Co-op
A program where you switch between classes and full-time paid jobs. Northeastern is the famous example.
Open curriculum
No required general classes. You pick almost everything. Brown and Amherst are known for it.
Consortium
A group of nearby schools that let students take classes at each other. The Five College Consortium in western Massachusetts is one.

Campus life

Major and minor
Your major is your main subject. A minor is a smaller second one. Most schools let you start undeclared.
Gen eds
Required classes outside your major, also called distribution requirements or the core.
Office hours
Set times when professors take questions. Going is one of the easiest ways to do better in a class.
RA
Resident assistant. An older student who lives on your floor, runs events and is the first person to go to with problems.
Meal plan
Prepaid dining hall meals. Many schools require one if you live on campus.
Greek life
Fraternities and sororities. Huge at some schools, banned or tiny at others. Rush is the recruiting period.
House system
Some schools put you in a smaller residential community you live and eat with for years, like Harvard's houses or Yale's residential colleges.
Commuter school
A school where many students live at home and drive or take transit in. Campus is quieter at night and on weekends.
Study abroad
Spending a semester or year taking classes in another country, usually junior year.

Rules and deadlines change and differ by school. Check each school's website before you apply.
